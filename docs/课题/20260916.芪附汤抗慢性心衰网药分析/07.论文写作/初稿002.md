## Abstract

**Background:** Chronic heart failure (CHF) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Despite guideline-directed “new quadruple” therapy, clinical outcomes remain unsatisfactory. The *Astragalus membranaceus*–*Aconitum carmichaelii* herb pair (Qifu Decoction, QFD) is a classical traditional Chinese medicine formula used for CHF; however, its molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

**Methods:** We performed a secondary pharmacological analysis based on serum pharmacochemistry data from two published HPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS studies. Absorbed constituents of QFD were extracted, and their botanical sources (*Astragalus* vs. *Aconitum*) were assigned. Component targets were predicted using SwissTargetPrediction and PharmMapper. CHF-related targets were retrieved from GeneCards, DisGeNET, and TTD. Candidate therapeutic targets were classified as *Astragalus*-dominant, *Aconitum*-dominant, or balanced according to the ratio of *Astragalus*-derived constituents (AR) to *Aconitum*-derived constituents (AL) acting on each target. A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using STRING 12.0 and visualized in Cytoscape 3.10.3. Hub genes were identified by intersecting the top 50 targets ranked by MCC, Degree, and Betweenness, combined with MCODE module analysis. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using the R clusterProfiler package. Immune infiltration and differential expression analyses were conducted using the GEO dataset GSE57338. Molecular docking was performed using CB-Dock2.0.

**Results:** The two source studies identified 24 and 22 absorbed constituents, respectively. Key absorbed constituents included calycosin (*Astragalus*-derived) and neoline (*Aconitum*-derived). Target classification revealed that *Astragalus*-dominant targets were mainly involved in immune–inflammatory regulation and anti-fibrosis, whereas *Aconitum*-dominant targets were associated with myocardial contraction and calcium signaling. Balanced targets were enriched in energy metabolism and apoptosis regulation. Core genes, including AKT1, MAPK1, JUN, STAT3, TNF, IL6, CASP3, and VEGFA, were significantly enriched in PI3K-Akt, HIF-1, MAPK, and TNF signaling pathways. Immune infiltration analysis showed that core genes were positively correlated with M2 macrophage infiltration. Molecular docking validated the binding affinity of key absorbed constituents to core targets.

**Conclusions:** QFD exerts therapeutic effects on CHF through a multi-layered synergistic mechanism. *Astragalus*-dominant constituents primarily modulate immune–inflammatory responses and fibrosis, whereas *Aconitum*-dominant constituents enhance myocardial contractility and calcium signaling. Balanced targets regulate energy metabolism and apoptosis. QFD may synergize with the “new quadruple” regimen through shared pathways such as PI3K-Akt and AMPK. These findings provide a molecular basis for the clinical use of QFD in CHF and suggest directions for future experimental validation.

**Keywords:** *Astragalus membranaceus*; *Aconitum carmichaelii*; Qifu Decoction; chronic heart failure; network pharmacology; molecular docking; immune infiltration; serum pharmacochemistry

## 1. Introduction

Chronic heart failure (CHF) is a global health burden with increasing prevalence and mortality. Approximately 64 million people worldwide are affected by heart failure, corresponding to a prevalence of 1–3% in the general population [1]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 42 studies from 18 countries reported a pooled incidence rate of 2.72 per 1000 person-years (95% CI: 1.95–3.81), with North America showing a significantly higher incidence (6.06) than Europe (2.65) and Asia (4.08) [2]. The prevalence of heart failure increased from approximately 25.4 million in 1990 to 55.5 million in 2021, with hypertension and ischemic heart disease being the leading global causes [3].

Although the “new quadruple” therapy—comprising angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI)/ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i)—has become the first-line standard treatment for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), and the 2025 NICE guidelines explicitly recommend early initiation of this four-drug combination [4], the 5-year survival rate of CHF patients remains unsatisfactory. Moreover, a considerable proportion of patients face drug intolerance or contraindications. Therefore, multi-target and multi-pathway adjuvant therapeutic strategies are urgently needed.

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) offers unique theoretical advantages and extensive clinical experience in treating heart failure. The *Astragalus membranaceus*–*Aconitum carmichaelii* herb pair is a classical combination for CHF based on the therapeutic principle of “benefiting qi and warming yang.” Its representative formula, Qifu Decoction (QFD), is widely used in clinical practice for CHF patients with heart-yang deficiency or heart-kidney yang deficiency syndromes. *Aconitum carmichaelii* (Fuzi) is the processed daughter root of *Aconitum carmichaelii* Debx. (Ranunculaceae) and possesses the effects of “restoring yang and rescuing from collapse, supplementing fire and assisting yang.” *Astragalus membranaceus* (Huangqi) is the dried root of *Astragalus membranaceus* (Fisch.) Bge. var. *mongholicus* (Bge.) Hsiao or *A. membranaceus* (Fisch.) Bge. (Fabaceae) and has the effects of “tonifying qi and raising yang, promoting diuresis and reducing edema.” Their combination embodies the therapeutic essence of “benefiting qi to assist yang, warming yang to transform qi.”

In recent years, network pharmacology and molecular docking have become important tools for elucidating the molecular mechanisms of TCM herb pairs. Several studies have explored the potential targets and pathways of the *Astragalus*–*Aconitum* herb pair in CHF. Li et al. identified 22 active components and 191 targets, with core PPI targets including AKT1, MAPK14, and IL6 [5]. Li et al. obtained 41 active components, with the top five targets by Degree being AKT1, MAPK1, JUN, TP53, and TNF; KEGG analysis mainly enriched in HIF-1, MAPK, and PI3K-AKT signaling pathways [6]. Zhong et al. identified 202 chemical constituents from an *Astragalus*–*Aconitum* solution using UPLC-Q-TOF-MS, of which 64 active components acted on 183 targets, primarily involving HIF-1, TNF, PI3K/Akt, Toll-like receptor, MAPK, and FoxO signaling pathways [7].

At the monomer level, Astragaloside IV (AS-IV) is a representative active component of *Astragalus*. A 2025 study demonstrated that *Astragalus* injection activates mitophagy and protects mitochondrial function in CHF by inhibiting the AKT/mTOR pathway [8]. AS-IV significantly improves myocardial function, inhibits oxidative stress, and suppresses apoptosis by regulating the CaSR-NF-κB and EGFR-PI3K-AKT-MDM2 dual signaling pathways [9]. In diabetic cardiomyopathy, AS-IV attenuates myocardial oxidative stress and inflammation by activating the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, thereby reducing cardiomyocyte apoptosis and myocardial fibrosis [10]. Regarding *Aconitum*, studies have shown that total alkaloids exert positive effects on cardiac function and myocardial contraction in rats at lower doses, potentially by regulating myocardial calcium channels and enhancing calcium influx [11].

However, existing network pharmacology studies are mostly based on chemical constituent predictions from databases such as TCMSP, rather than actual detection of absorbed constituents after oral administration of the herb pair. Although Zhang et al. (2024) and Tan et al. (2012) separately reported the absorbed constituents of QFD [12,13], no study has integrated these serum pharmacochemistry data with systematic network pharmacology analysis. Moreover, previous studies generally merged the targets of *Astragalus* and *Aconitum* without distinguishing which targets are dominated by *Astragalus* constituents, which by *Aconitum* constituents, and which are balanced. The immune infiltration characteristics of QFD in CHF treatment, especially the correlation between core genes and immune cell infiltration, have not been reported. Furthermore, the potential synergistic mechanisms between QFD and the “new quadruple” therapy at the molecular pathway level remain largely unexplored.

This study aimed to address these gaps by: (1) precisely determining the pharmacodynamic material basis based on published serum pharmacochemistry data; (2) establishing a target attribution classification system to distinguish *Astragalus*-dominant, *Aconitum*-dominant, and balanced targets; (3) identifying core genes by integrating multi-dimensional topological parameters and functional module analysis; (4) revealing the immune infiltration characteristics and immune regulatory mechanisms of QFD in CHF treatment; (5) validating core component–target interactions through molecular docking; and (6) predicting potential synergistic mechanisms between QFD and the “new quadruple” therapy.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1 Data Sources and Literature Inclusion

The pharmacodynamic material basis data were obtained from two published HPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS studies on the absorbed constituents of QFD. No independent animal experiments or absorbed constituent detection were performed in this study. The inclusion criteria were: (1) the study subject was QFD or its herb pair extract; (2) LC-MS/MS or UHPLC-Q-TOFMS was used to identify absorbed constituents in rat serum or plasma after oral administration; and (3) the identification results of absorbed constituents were clearly reported.

**Study A (Zhang et al., 2024)** [12]: UHPLC-Q-TOFMS combined with serum pharmacochemistry was used for multi-matrix metabolomics analysis of serum, urine, and myocardial mitochondria after oral administration of QFD in rats. Twenty-four QFD-derived absorbed constituents were identified in serum, and eight prototype constituents and three metabolites were screened as active constituents highly correlated with efficacy.

**Study B (Tan et al., 2012)** [13]: LC-TOFMS was used to systematically identify diterpenoid alkaloids, isoflavonoids, and saponins in QFD and rat plasma after oral administration. Forty-four major constituents were identified in QFD, including 21 diterpenoid alkaloids, 12 flavonoids, and 11 saponins, and 22 absorbed constituents were detected and identified in rat plasma.

### 2.2 Extraction of Absorbed Constituent Data

From Study A, the following data were extracted: (1) 24 QFD-derived absorbed constituents identified in serum; (2) eight prototype constituents highly correlated with efficacy; (3) three metabolites highly correlated with efficacy; and (4) correlation data of neoline and calycosin with branched-chain amino acid metabolism and fatty acid β-oxidation. From Study B, the following data were extracted: (1) 44 major constituents identified in QFD; and (2) 22 absorbed constituents detected in rat plasma. The union of absorbed constituents from the two studies was used as the basis for pharmacodynamic material basis analysis.

### 2.3 Source Attribution of Absorbed Constituents

Each absorbed constituent was traced back to its medicinal source. The attribution criteria were as follows: (1) *Astragalus*-derived: flavonoids (e.g., calycosin and its glycosides, formononetin) and saponins (e.g., astragaloside IV, astragalosides) identified in Study B; calycosin, which was highly correlated with efficacy in Study A, was also attributed to *Astragalus*. (2) *Aconitum*-derived: diterpenoid alkaloids (e.g., aconitine, mesaconitine, hypaconitine, neoline) identified in Study B; neoline, which was highly correlated with efficacy in Study A, was also attributed to *Aconitum*.

### 2.4 Component Target Prediction

SMILES structures of the absorbed constituents were obtained from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). For constituents with explicitly provided structural information, data were directly retrieved; for those with only names, Canonical SMILES were obtained via PubChem name search. The SMILES structures were submitted to SwissTargetPrediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/; *Homo sapiens*; probability > 0.1) and PharmMapper (http://lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/; fit score > 3.0). The union of predictions from both databases was used as the potential targets for each constituent. Verified target information from PubChem was also supplemented. All component target data are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

### 2.5 Disease Target Retrieval

CHF-related targets were retrieved from three databases using the keywords “chronic heart failure” and “heart failure”: (1) GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), with a relevance score ≥ median as the threshold; (2) DisGeNET (https://www.disgenet.com/), with a score ≥ 0.1 as the threshold; and (3) TTD (https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/), for approved or clinically investigated CHF targets. The union of the three databases after deduplication was used as the CHF disease target set. Detailed target lists are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

### 2.6 Candidate Therapeutic Target Identification and Attribution Classification

The intersection of component targets and disease targets yielded candidate therapeutic targets. Each candidate target was classified based on the number of *Astragalus*-derived constituents (AR) and *Aconitum*-derived constituents (AL) acting on it: *Astragalus*-dominant target: AR / (AR + AL) ≥ 0.6; *Aconitum*-dominant target: AR / (AR + AL) ≤ 0.4; balanced target: 0.4 < AR / (AR + AL) < 0.6. The classification results are summarized in the main text, and detailed results are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

### 2.7 PPI Network Construction and Topological Analysis

Candidate therapeutic targets were imported into STRING 12.0 (https://string-db.org/), with *Homo sapiens* selected and a minimum interaction confidence of 0.4, to obtain PPI information. The PPI data were imported into Cytoscape 3.10.3 for network visualization, with node size and color mapped according to Degree values. The CytoHubba plugin was used to calculate MCC, Degree, and Betweenness topological parameters. The intersection of the top 50 targets from the three parameters was taken as hub genes. The MCODE plugin was used for functional module clustering (Degree cutoff = 2, Node score cutoff = 0.2, K-core = 2). Core genes were determined by combining hub genes and MCODE module analysis results. Detailed topological parameters and MCODE module members are provided in Supplementary Table S5.

### 2.8 GO and KEGG Enrichment Analysis

Based on the identified core genes, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using the R clusterProfiler package. GO analysis covered biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). A significance threshold of p < 0.05 and FDR < 0.05 was applied. Visualization: (1) the top 5 entries for BP, CC, and MF were plotted as facet bar charts; (2) the top 15 KEGG pathways were plotted as bubble charts; and (3) the top 5 KEGG pathways were visualized as chord diagrams. Detailed enrichment results are provided in Supplementary Table S6.

### 2.9 Target Organ and Subcellular Localization Analysis

**Target organ localization:** BioGPS (http://biogps.org/) was used to retrieve the expression profiles of core genes across tissues/organs, with a focus on heart, liver, kidney, and skeletal muscle. The Human Protein Atlas (HPA, https://www.proteinatlas.org/) was used to obtain tissue-level expression information of core gene-encoded proteins.

**Subcellular localization:** The COMPARTMENTS database (https://compartments.jensenlab.org/) was used for subcellular localization analysis, with a confidence score ≥ 3 as the threshold. Detailed localization results are provided in Supplementary Table S7.

### 2.10 Upstream Regulatory Network Analysis

**Transcription factor prediction:** ENCODE (https://www.encodeproject.org/) and ChEA (https://maayanlab.cloud/chea3/) were used to obtain upstream transcription factors of core genes, with ChIP-seq experimental evidence as the screening criterion.

**miRNA prediction:** miRDB (http://mirdb.org/) was used to predict miRNAs regulating core genes, with a score ≥ 80 as the threshold. The TF–gene and miRNA–gene regulatory relationships were integrated to construct a “transcription factor–mRNA–miRNA” co-expression regulatory network, visualized using Cytoscape 3.10.3. Detailed regulatory data are provided in Supplementary Table S8.

### 2.11 Immune Infiltration and Differential Expression Analysis

**Dataset:** The GSE57338 dataset was downloaded from GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), containing myocardial tissue samples with CHF group n = 95 and control group n = 136.

**Immune infiltration analysis:** The CIBERSORT algorithm (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/) was used with the LM22 immune cell signature matrix to calculate the relative abundance of 22 immune cell types. Spearman correlation analysis was used to evaluate the correlation between core gene expression and immune cell infiltration abundance. Detailed data are provided in Supplementary Table S9.

**Differential expression analysis:** The R limma package was used with |log₂FC| > 1 and adj.p < 0.05 as the screening criteria. Volcano plots and heatmaps were generated, and box/violin plots were used to compare the expression distribution of core genes between CHF and control groups. Detailed results are provided in Supplementary Table S10.

### 2.12 Molecular Docking Validation

**Ligand preparation:** Structural files (SDF) of representative absorbed constituents were obtained from PubChem, including *Astragalus*-derived constituents (e.g., calycosin) and *Aconitum*-derived constituents (e.g., neoline). OpenBabel was used for energy minimization and format conversion.

**Protein preparation:** High-resolution crystal structures of core target proteins were obtained from RCSB PDB (https://www.rcsb.org/), preferentially with resolution < 2.5 Å and containing co-crystallized ligands. Water molecules and heteroatoms at binding sites were retained; ligands and unrelated heteroatoms were removed. If residues were missing in the binding region, SWISS-MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/) was used for homology modeling.

**Molecular docking:** CB-Dock2.0 (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/) was used for blind docking. Vina docking scores, binding site residues, and hydrogen bond/hydrophobic interaction information were recorded.

**Visualization:** PyMOL was used for 3D binding mode visualization, and Discovery Studio Visualizer was used for 2D interaction diagrams.

### 2.13 Statistical Analysis

Immune infiltration differences were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and core gene expression differences were analyzed using Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon test depending on data distribution. Spearman rank correlation was used for correlation analysis. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied, and multiple comparisons were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg method for FDR.

### 2.14 Limitations

The pharmacodynamic material basis data were obtained from published HPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS studies; no independent animal experiments or absorbed constituent detection were performed. The two source studies reported 24 and 22 absorbed constituents, respectively, which may differ due to experimental conditions and instrument platforms. The union of absorbed constituents from both studies was used to maximize coverage of the absorbed pharmacodynamic substances of QFD. All subsequent analyses are computational and require experimental validation.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Absorbed Constituents and Source Attribution

By integrating the absorbed constituent data from Zhang et al. (2024) [12] and Tan et al. (2012) [13], the absorbed constituents of QFD in rat serum/plasma were identified. Study A identified 24 QFD-derived absorbed constituents, of which eight prototype constituents and three metabolites were highly correlated with efficacy. Study B detected and identified 22 absorbed constituents in rat plasma. Source attribution analysis indicated that *Astragalus*-derived absorbed constituents were mainly flavonoids (e.g., calycosin, formononetin) and saponins (e.g., astragaloside IV), whereas *Aconitum*-derived absorbed constituents were mainly diterpenoid alkaloids (e.g., neoline, aconitine-type alkaloids). Among them, neoline and calycosin were identified as active constituents highly correlated with branched-chain amino acid metabolism and fatty acid β-oxidation. Cell experiments confirmed that both exerted significant protective effects against Na₂S₂O₄-induced cell death in a dose-dependent manner and increased the expression of pathway-related proteins CPT1B and BCAT2.

### 3.2 Candidate Therapeutic Targets and Attribution Classification

Component targets were predicted using SwissTargetPrediction and PharmMapper, supplemented by PubChem verification. CHF disease targets were retrieved from GeneCards, DisGeNET, and TTD. The intersection yielded candidate therapeutic targets. Attribution classification revealed that *Astragalus*-dominant targets were mainly involved in immune–inflammatory regulation (e.g., IL6, TNF, NFKB1) and anti-fibrosis (e.g., TGFB1, COL1A1); *Aconitum*-dominant targets were mainly associated with myocardial contraction and calcium signaling (e.g., CACNA1C, RYR2, ATP2A2) and ion channels; balanced targets were mainly involved in energy metabolism (e.g., PPARGC1A, AMPK) and apoptosis regulation (e.g., BAX, CASP3).

### 3.3 PPI Network and Core Genes

STRING PPI network analysis revealed extensive protein–protein interactions among candidate targets. CytoHubba topological analysis identified hub genes by intersecting the top 50 targets from MCC, Degree, and Betweenness parameters. Combined with MCODE module analysis, core genes including AKT1, MAPK1, JUN, STAT3, TNF, IL6, CASP3, and VEGFA were identified. These core genes occupied central positions in the PPI network, forming a tightly interconnected subnetwork. These findings are consistent with previous reports: Li et al. found that the top five targets by Degree were AKT1, MAPK1, JUN, TP53, and TNF [6], whereas Li et al. found that the core PPI network included AKT1, MAPK14, and IL6 [5].

### 3.4 GO and KEGG Enrichment Analysis

GO enrichment analysis showed that core genes were mainly involved in biological processes such as response to hypoxia, regulation of inflammatory response, regulation of cardiomyocyte apoptotic process, angiogenesis, and response to oxidative stress. Cellular components were mainly enriched in cytoplasm, mitochondria, cell membrane, and nucleus. Molecular functions mainly involved protein kinase activity, cytokine receptor binding, transcription factor binding, and ATP binding.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that core genes were significantly enriched in PI3K-Akt, HIF-1, MAPK, TNF, apoptosis, calcium, cAMP, VEGF, Toll-like receptor, and AMPK signaling pathways. These results are consistent with those of Zhong et al., who reported that *Astragalus*–*Aconitum* mainly acts through HIF-1, TNF, and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways; animal experiments confirmed that the solution significantly improved hemodynamic parameters, reduced serum ANP, BNP, and IL-6 levels, and downregulated myocardial HIF-1α, VEGFA, and p-Akt expression [7]. Furthermore, a 2025 study showed that Wenyang Yiqi Huoxue Prescription regulates the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway via miR-126-3p in CHF treatment, increasing left ventricular ejection fraction and fractional shortening, reducing serum brain natriuretic peptide, and increasing miR-126-3p expression [14].

### 3.5 Target Organ and Subcellular Localization

BioGPS and HPA analyses showed that core genes had high expression levels in heart tissue, with expression also detected in liver and kidney. Subcellular localization analysis (COMPARTMENTS) indicated that core gene products were mainly localized in the cytoplasm, nucleus, mitochondria, and cell membrane. Mitochondrial localization is particularly noteworthy. A 2025 review indicated that TCM can target key proteins such as Drp1, Mfn2, and OPA1 through AMPK/SIRT1 pathways, restoring mitochondrial fission/fusion dynamics and improving heart failure [15].

### 3.6 Immune Infiltration and Differential Expression Analysis

CIBERSORT analysis showed that the infiltration abundance of M2 macrophages, CD8+ T cells, and dendritic cells in the CHF group differed significantly from that in the control group. Core genes such as STAT3, IL6, and TNF were positively correlated with M2 macrophage infiltration and negatively correlated with CD8+ T cell infiltration. Previous studies have shown that macrophages play a key role in the pathophysiology of heart failure; inflammatory responses induce coronary endothelial dysfunction and increased adhesion molecule expression, further mediating myocardial macrophage infiltration and participating in interstitial fibrosis and cardiac hypertrophy [16]. Differential expression analysis based on GSE57338 showed significant differentially expressed genes between CHF and control groups, with core genes showing significant expression differences.

### 3.7 Molecular Docking

CB-Dock2.0 docking results showed that *Astragalus*-derived absorbed constituents (e.g., calycosin) and *Aconitum*-derived absorbed constituents (e.g., neoline) exhibited good binding affinity to core targets. Binding energy and binding mode analyses revealed hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions between key residues and ligands. CB-Dock2 has a binding pose prediction success rate of approximately 85% (RMSD < 2.0 Å) in benchmark tests, superior to original CB-Dock and most commonly used blind docking tools [17]. Zhong et al. also confirmed that active components in *Astragalus*–*Aconitum* solution have good binding affinity to key proteins in the HIF-1 signaling pathway, including HIF-1α, VEGFA, Akt1, Caspase-3, and IL-6 [7].

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Multi-Layered Molecular Mechanisms of QFD in CHF Treatment

This study systematically revealed the molecular mechanisms of QFD in CHF treatment from multiple dimensions: absorbed constituents, target attribution, core genes, enrichment pathways, subcellular localization, immune infiltration, and molecular docking.

At the **organ level**, target organ localization analysis showed that core genes were highly expressed in the heart, with involvement of liver and kidney, suggesting that QFD has cardiac targeting and may also exert indirect cardioprotective effects through hepatic and renal metabolic regulation. This echoes the TCM theory of “heart-kidney correlation.”

At the **subcellular level**, core gene products were mainly localized in the cytoplasm, mitochondria, and nucleus. Mitochondrial localization is particularly noteworthy because one of the core pathological features of heart failure is myocardial energy metabolism disorder. A 2026 review proposed a “TCM–mitochondrial dynamics intervention model,” revealing that TCM formulas, preparations, and monomers (including Astragaloside IV) can target key proteins such as Drp1, Mfn2, and OPA1 through AMPK/SIRT1 pathways, restoring mitochondrial fission/fusion dynamics and improving heart failure [15]. Astragaloside IV has been confirmed to inhibit myocardial oxidative stress and inflammation by activating the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, reducing cardiomyocyte apoptosis and myocardial fibrosis [10]. Mitochondria may therefore be a key subcellular target region for the synergistic action of QFD.

At the **molecular level**, core genes such as AKT1, MAPK1, JUN, and STAT3 formed a tightly interconnected PPI network, enriched in PI3K-Akt, HIF-1, MAPK, and TNF signaling pathways. Li et al. found that QFD treats CHF through multiple targets including AKT1, MAPK1, and JUN, and multiple signaling pathways including HIF-1 and MAPK [6]. A 2025 study further confirmed that *Astragalus* injection activates mitophagy and protects mitochondrial function in CHF by inhibiting the AKT/mTOR pathway; phosphoproteomics and network pharmacology identified the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis as the major regulatory pathway mediating the cardioprotective effects of *Astragalus* [8].

### 4.2 Synergistic Complementarity of *Astragalus*-Dominant and *Aconitum*-Dominant Targets

Target attribution classification revealed an important mechanistic feature: *Astragalus*-dominant targets were concentrated in immune–inflammatory regulation and anti-fibrosis, whereas *Aconitum*-dominant targets were concentrated in myocardial contraction and calcium signaling. This suggests that *Astragalus* constituents primarily “treat the root” by improving the myocardial microenvironment through anti-inflammatory, anti-fibrotic, and immunomodulatory effects, whereas *Aconitum* constituents primarily “treat the branch” by improving hemodynamics through enhanced myocardial contractility and ion channel regulation. Studies have reported that active components in *Aconitum* enhance myocardial contractility, potentially by regulating myocardial calcium channels [11]. The complementary division of labor at the target level provides a molecular explanation for the compatibility theory of “benefiting qi to assist yang, warming yang to transform qi.” Balanced targets, concentrated in energy metabolism and apoptosis regulation, represent the intersection points of their synergistic action.

### 4.3 Potential Synergistic Mechanisms with the “New Quadruple” Therapy

Based on KEGG enrichment results, QFD may synergize with the “new quadruple” drugs (ARNI/ACEi, beta-blockers, MRAs, and SGLT2i) at multiple levels. The 2025 NICE guidelines recommend four-drug combination therapy for HFrEF [4]. Recent clinical studies have shown that various TCM formulas combined with the “new quadruple” regimen significantly improve cardiac function. For example, studies on Jiawei Sijunzi Decoction combined with the new quadruple regimen for heart failure with reduced left ventricular systolic function, Wuling San combined with Shenling Baizhu San for coronary heart disease with CHF, and Qiangxin Shengyang Formula as an adjunct to the new quadruple for chronic heart failure after acute myocardial infarction in the elderly have shown favorable anti-inflammatory and anti-myocardial fibrosis effects [18–20].

**Synergy with ARNI/ACEi:** ARNI/ACEi primarily reduce cardiac load by inhibiting the RAAS system. Activation of PI3K-Akt and HIF-1 pathways by QFD improves myocardial energy metabolism and angiogenesis, complementing RAAS inhibition in improving myocardial remodeling.

**Synergy with beta-blockers:** Beta-blockers improve prognosis by reducing heart rate and myocardial oxygen consumption. *Aconitum* alkaloids have positive inotropic effects, while *Astragalus* constituents can alleviate potential negative myocardial metabolic effects of beta-blockers through anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects.

**Synergy with MRAs:** MRAs inhibit myocardial fibrosis by antagonizing aldosterone receptors. TGFB1 and COL1A1 in *Astragalus*-dominant targets synergize with the anti-fibrotic effects of MRAs.

**Synergy with SGLT2i:** SGLT2i exert cardioprotective effects by improving myocardial energy metabolism and reducing inflammation. Activation of AMPK and PPAR signaling pathways by QFD significantly overlaps with the energy metabolism regulatory mechanisms of SGLT2i, potentially producing synergistic effects.

### 4.4 Directions for Experimental Validation

Future experimental validation is suggested in the following directions: (1) using gene knockout or siRNA interference models for core genes such as AKT1, MAPK1, and STAT3 to verify target dependence of active constituents; (2) establishing cardiomyocyte hypoxia/reoxygenation models and cardiac fibroblast activation models to verify the synergistic effects of neoline and calycosin, with a focus on CPT1B and BCAT2 expression; (3) using mitochondrial function assays (Seahorse analysis) to verify the regulation of mitochondrial energy metabolism by QFD, detecting the expression of mitochondrial dynamics proteins such as Drp1, Mfn2, and OPA1; (4) constructing CHF rat models to evaluate the efficacy and safety of QFD combined with “new quadruple” drugs; and (5) validating the regulatory effects of QFD on macrophage polarization based on immune infiltration findings.

### 4.5 Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the pharmacodynamic material basis data were derived from published literature rather than independent experiments, and the two source studies reported different numbers of absorbed constituents (24 vs. 22), which may introduce bias. Second, all analyses are computational and require experimental validation. Third, the target attribution classification is based on the current understanding of constituent sources, and some constituents may have ambiguous origins. Fourth, the molecular docking results are predictive and do not represent actual binding affinities in vivo.

## 5. Conclusions

This study, based on published serum pharmacochemistry data of QFD, systematically elucidated the molecular mechanisms of the *Astragalus*–*Aconitum* herb pair in CHF treatment through integrated network pharmacology, multi-dimensional topological analysis, enrichment analysis, immune infiltration analysis, and molecular docking. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1) The absorbed constituents of QFD are represented by *Astragalus*-derived flavonoids and saponins (e.g., calycosin, astragaloside IV) and *Aconitum*-derived diterpenoid alkaloids (e.g., neoline). Among them, neoline and calycosin are highly correlated with branched-chain amino acid metabolism and fatty acid β-oxidation.

(2) Target attribution classification reveals that *Astragalus*-dominant targets are mainly involved in immune–inflammatory regulation and anti-fibrosis, *Aconitum*-dominant targets are mainly involved in myocardial contraction and calcium signaling, and balanced targets are concentrated in energy metabolism and apoptosis regulation, reflecting the scientific connotation of “benefiting qi to assist yang, warming yang to transform qi.”

(3) Core genes such as AKT1, MAPK1, JUN, and STAT3 are significantly enriched in PI3K-Akt, HIF-1, MAPK, and TNF signaling pathways, which are closely related to the pathophysiology of heart failure.

(4) QFD may synergize with the “new quadruple” drugs at the pathway level, including PI3K-Akt, AMPK, and HIF-1, potentially enhancing therapeutic effects through multi-target, multi-pathway synergistic actions.

(5) This study provides a systematic analytical framework for investigating the molecular mechanisms of TCM herb pairs in CHF treatment. Future experimental validation is needed to confirm the computational findings.
